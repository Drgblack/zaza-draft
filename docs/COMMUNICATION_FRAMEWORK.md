Zaza Draft Communication Framework
The principles behind every draft
Version 1.0 — March 2026

Why this exists
Teacher-to-parent communication is one of the highest-stakes writing tasks in a school. A poorly worded message can escalate a concern into a complaint, damage a relationship built over months, or leave a parent feeling dismissed at exactly the moment they most need to feel heard. A well-worded message can de-escalate tension, reinforce trust, and keep the focus where it belongs — on the child.
Zaza Draft exists to help teachers write those messages well, even when they are tired, upset, or under time pressure. Every output the product generates is shaped by the principles in this document.

Four principles that govern every draft
1. Calm and professional
The teacher's message must never sound defensive, reactive, or blame-laden — regardless of how the original input was written or how provocative the parent's message was. A professional teacher communicates from a position of measured authority, not emotional reactivity.
This means Zaza Draft will always soften inflammatory language, remove threats and ultimatums, and reframe harsh observations into factual, constructive statements — without losing the substance of what the teacher needs to say.
2. Genuinely empathetic
Empathy in teacher communication is not a formulaic opener. It is a genuine acknowledgement of the child's experience and the parent's concern, written in a way that feels human rather than procedural.
Zaza Draft distinguishes between real empathy and performed empathy. Phrases like "I know this will feel serious" or "I understand you must be worried" tell the parent how they feel rather than acknowledging how they feel. Real empathy sounds like: "I'm truly sorry to hear Jake had such a difficult day" or "I completely understand why this is worrying for you both."
The difference matters. Parents can tell.
3. Clear and action-oriented
Every parent-facing message should leave the parent knowing at least one concrete thing that will happen next. Vague commitments like "I will look into this" are insufficient for high-concern cases. The more serious the concern, the more specific the next step needs to be.
For standard updates this might be: "I will go through the missing homework with her in class tomorrow and make the next deadline clear."
For high-risk incidents this means naming the investigation steps: who the teacher will speak with, when, and what they will do with what they learn.
4. Timely and relational
Written communication has limits. Zaza Draft recognises that some conversations — particularly around safety incidents, repeated behavioural concerns, or emotionally charged complaints — should not be resolved by email alone. Where appropriate, drafts should invite a phone call or in-person meeting rather than attempting to close the matter in writing.
This is not a weakness. It is professional judgment.

Message types and what each requires
Standard parent update
Used for: homework, attendance patterns, general progress, positive news
Structure:

Brief, natural opening (not formulaic)
The specific concern or update, grounded in what the teacher actually observed
One concrete next step the teacher will take in school
Optional: invitation for the parent to reinforce at home
Professional sign-off

Tone: warm but efficient. Parents are busy. A well-crafted four-sentence message is more valuable than a sprawling paragraph.
What to avoid: over-explaining, hedging, unnecessary softening that obscures the actual message.

Parent complaint response
Used for: parent raising a concern, expressing dissatisfaction, asking for explanation
Structure:

Acknowledge receipt of the message genuinely (one sentence, not procedural)
Acknowledge the child's experience and the parent's concern specifically
State what you know so far and what you will investigate
Name the concrete next steps
Offer a real conversation (phone or in-person) for anything complex
Professional sign-off

Tone: calm, empathetic, transparent. Not defensive.
What to avoid:

Restating the parent's own information back to them
Telling the parent how they feel
Over-promising specific outcomes
Treating the email as the resolution when a conversation is needed


High-risk incident response
Used for: physical incidents, bullying reports, safety concerns, child distress, repeated pattern complaints
This is the highest-stakes category. The framework here is non-negotiable.
Step 1 — Validate emotion genuinely
Open by acknowledging the child's experience and the parent's worry, warmly and specifically. Use the child's name. Do not open with a procedural statement.
Right: "I'm really sorry to hear Jake had such a difficult experience today. I completely understand why this is worrying and upsetting for both of you."
Wrong: "I know this will feel serious."
Wrong: "I wanted to follow up on what happened today."
Step 2 — Be transparent without being defensive
If you did not witness the incident, say so honestly — but make clear that you take the report seriously regardless.
Right: "I did not personally witness a push during class today, but that does not mean it didn't happen, and I take your report very seriously."
Wrong: "I was watching the whole time."
Wrong: "Children sometimes perceive things differently."
Step 3 — Commit to concrete investigation
Name the actual steps you will take: speak with the child, speak with others involved, speak with any witnesses. Do not over-promise outcomes.
Right: "I will speak with Jake privately tomorrow morning to hear his account, and I will also speak with the other students involved to get a full picture of what occurred."
Wrong: "I will look into this."
Wrong: "This will never happen again."
Step 4 — Propose a real conversation
High-risk cases should not be resolved by email. Offer a phone call or in-person meeting with a specific suggested time.
Right: "Would you be available for a short phone call tomorrow afternoon, or would you prefer to meet in person at a time that suits you?"
Wrong: "Please don't hesitate to reach out if you have further questions."
Step 5 — Reinforce partnership
Close briefly by affirming that working together is how you support the child. Keep it genuine, not formulaic.
Right: "Working together is the most effective way to make sure Jake feels safe and supported at school."
Wrong: "Thank you for your patience in this matter."

Report comment
Used for: end-of-term or mid-term written reports
Structure:

No greeting
No sign-off
Factual, specific observations about the student's performance and behaviour
Forward-looking statement where appropriate
Uses correct pronouns for the student throughout

Tone: professional, specific, and honest. Report comments should reflect reality, not just positivity. A comment that mentions both strengths and areas for growth is more credible and more useful to the parent than unqualified praise.
What to avoid: greeting or email structure bleeding into the comment, awkward pronoun handling for ambiguous names, generic phrases that could apply to any student.

What Zaza Draft will never produce
Regardless of what a teacher types, Zaza Draft will not generate output that:

Threatens a student or parent
Dismisses a parent's concern as overreaction
Makes promises about outcomes the teacher cannot control
Tells a parent how they feel
Parrots the parent's own information back to them
Uses language that could expose the teacher or school to complaint or liability
Sounds like it was written by a system rather than a person


A note on tone controls
Zaza Draft offers four tone settings — Warm, Professional, Direct, and Empathetic. These are genuine variations, not cosmetic changes.
Warm is slightly softer and more relational, inviting partnership with the parent more explicitly.
Professional is measured, factual, and balanced — the default for most school communication.
Direct is more concise and explicit about the issue and expectation, with less softening language.
Empathetic leads more strongly with emotional acknowledgement before moving to practical steps, and is particularly suited to high-concern situations.
All four tones stay within the bounds of professional teacher communication. None of them produces output that sounds like a support bot, a legal disclaimer, or a corporate apology.

The standard the product is held to
Every draft Zaza Draft produces should pass a simple test: could a confident, experienced, professionally trained teacher send this message without editing it?
If the answer is yes, the draft has done its job.
If the answer is no — because it sounds formulaic, generic, defensive, or machine-generated — it has not.
That is the standard this framework is built around.
